Educational explainer, not a prediction of an imminent eruption. 

Mount Rainier is at USGS Volcano Alert Level NORMAL / Aviation Color Code GREEN as of the Cascade Range update of July 24, 2026. Sources for every figure and claim in the video:
Current status and the July 2025 earthquake swarm
• Mount Rainier at Volcano Alert Level NORMAL, Aviation Color Code GREEN; monitoring "consistent with background activity levels in the Cascade Range" (USGS Cascade Range Volcano Weekly Update, July 24, 2026)
https://volcanoes.usgs.gov/hans-public/volcano/cas
• Mount Rainier volcano status page, current alert level (USGS HANS)
https://volcanoes.usgs.gov/hans-public/volcano/wa6
• Swarm onset at 1:29 a.m. PDT on July 8, 2025 near the summit; "there is no indication that the level of earthquake activity is cause for concern"; alert level unchanged at NORMAL/GREEN (USGS Volcano Notice, July 8, 2025)
https://volcanoes.usgs.gov/hans-public/notice/DOI-USGS-CVO-2025-07-08T14:41:41+00:00
• Final swarm tally: over 1,350 located earthquakes plus thousands too small to locate; maximum magnitude 2.4 on July 11, 2025; activity "related to the volcano's hydrothermal system"; helicopter gas survey of August 4, 2025 found emissions consistent with historical measurements; no unusual ground deformation; no anomalous infrasound; the 2025 swarm "surpasses the 2009 swarm in terms of total events, event rate, and energy release" (USGS Volcano Notice, August 25, 2025)
https://volcanoes.usgs.gov/hans-public/notice/DOI-USGS-CVO-2025-08-25T22:19:13+00:00
• Peak rate of roughly 40 earthquakes per hour against a background of about 10 per month; depths mainly 1.5–4 miles (2–6 km) beneath the summit; "the most likely cause of the earthquakes is water moving around the crust above the magma chamber"; no earthquakes felt at the surface (USGS Cascades Volcano Observatory)
https://www.usgs.gov/observatories/cvo/news/monitoring-stations-detect-small-magnitude-earthquakes-mount-rainier
• "Relatively minor changes in the hydrothermal (hot water) system at the core of the volcano have generated enough localized stress changes to break rock" (Pacific Northwest Seismic Network, Mount Rainier earthquake swarm of July 2025)
https://pnsn.org/blog/mount-rainier-earthquake-swarm-of-july-2025
• Rainier typically experiences one to two earthquake swarms per year; swarms attributed to hydrothermal fluids moving along existing faults in the basement rock (USGS, Earthquake Monitoring at Mount Rainier)
https://www.usgs.gov/volcanoes/mount-rainier/science/earthquake-monitoring-mount-rainier
• Evidence for fluid-triggered slip in the 2009 Mount Rainier earthquake swarm (USGS publication)
https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70187065
The threat ranking, and the honest Yellowstone comparison
• 2018 Update to the U.S. Geological Survey National Volcanic Threat Assessment: 161 U.S. volcanoes scored on 15 hazard factors and 9 exposure factors; Mount Rainier ranks 3rd with a threat score of 203 in the "very high threat" tier (behind only Kilauea, 263, and Mount St. Helens, 235); Yellowstone caldera ranks 21st with a score of 115 in the "high threat" tier; 18 volcanoes score above 128 as very high threat (Ewert, Diefenbach & Ramsey, USGS SIR 2018-5140)
https://pubs.usgs.gov/sir/2018/5140/sir20185140.pdf
• USGS news release on the 2018 National Volcanic Threat Assessment
https://www.usgs.gov/news/volcanic-threat-assessment-help-prioritize-risk-reduction-efforts-us-volcanoes
• Yellowstone: "the yearly probability of another caldera-forming eruption can be approximated as 1 in 730,000"; "there is no evidence that a catastrophic eruption at Yellowstone is imminent, and such events are unlikely to occur in the next few centuries"; roughly 80 relatively nonexplosive eruptions, primarily lava flows, in the 640,000 years since the last giant eruption, and such an eruption "would likely threaten few lives" (USGS, Questions About Supervolcanoes)
https://www.usgs.gov/volcanoes/yellowstone/questions-about-supervolcanoes
• Full 2018 threat-assessment ranking table for all 161 volcanoes (reference copy)

Why lahars, not lava or ash, are the hazard at Mount Rainier
• Mount Rainier "Living Safely With a Volcano in Your Backyard": lahars are "rapidly flowing slurries of mud and boulders" that "destroy or bury most manmade structures in their paths"; hydrothermal alteration converts hard volcanic rock into weak, clay-rich rock, and "when masses of water-saturated clay-rich rock slide away, they transform rapidly into a lahar"; the west flank including the head of the Puyallup River has the greatest potential for far-travelled lahars, with the Nisqually valley at lesser risk; past lahars travelled 45 to 50 mph and were up to 100 feet thick in confined valleys near the volcano, slowing to 15 to 25 mph downstream; lava and pyroclastic flows are unlikely to travel farther than 10 miles from the summit and would stay within the national park; ash would be distributed downwind, mostly east, away from Puget Sound; more than one cubic mile of glacial ice across 25 glaciers, "as much as all other Cascade Range volcanoes combined"; large lahars reach the Puget Sound lowland on average at least once every 500 to 1,000 years, roughly a 1-in-10 chance during an average human lifespan; "there may be little or no advance warning" and in a rare non-eruptive case "the only warning could be a report that a lahar is already underway"; "lahars are almost always preceded by volcanic unrest, so in most instances there will be time to warn people"; a large lahar in the upper Puyallup valley without precursors "could arrive at the City of Orting as little as 40 minutes after the initial warning is sounded"; Rainier "has erupted less often and less explosively in recent millennia than its well-known neighbor, Mount St. Helens" yet the proximity of population in lahar valleys makes it "a far greater threat to life and property"; Electron Mudflow deposits as much as 20 feet deep at Orting; dams and reservoirs could either lessen or increase a lahar's extent; Armero, Colombia, 1985 (Driedger & Scott, USGS Fact Sheet 2008-3062)
https://pubs.usgs.gov/fs/2008/3062/fs2008-3062.pdf
• Mount Rainier Volcanic Hazard Information, the current public hazard brochure: "evidence shows that at least one past lahar at Mount Rainier began with no known eruption. A lahar without any volcanic activity to serve as a warning is known locally as a 'no-notice' lahar"; "very large lahars can also form when weak and water-saturated rock high on the volcano collapses with or without volcanic activity"; estimated arrival times for a worst-case no-notice lahar starting on the west side — Orting about 1 hour, Sumner 2 hours, Puyallup 3 hours, Fife 5 hours; "be prepared to evacuate on foot, not by car. Roads can quickly become clogged during a sudden evacuation"; siren coverage along the Carbon, Puyallup and Nisqually rivers (USGS General Information Product 265, April 2026)
https://pubs.usgs.gov/gip/265/gip265.pdf
• Modeling the dynamics of lahars that originate as landslides on the west side of Mount Rainier: "no geologic evidence discovered to date has linked the Electron Mudflow with eruptive activity," described as an "unheralded lahar"; Electron volume about 260 million cubic metres, travelling more than 50 km downstream; worst-case scenario S-260-HM from Sunset Amphitheater produces a flow front about 4 metres deep travelling about 4 metres per second that "reaches the Orting lowlands about 1 hour after the onset of slope failure," then "slows down and comes to rest in the valleys surrounding Sumner and Puyallup"; the Tahoma Glacier headwall is the sector "most prone to large-scale gravitational collapse"; eight scenarios modelled with the D-Claw numerical model (USGS Open-File Report 2021-1118)
https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/2021/1118/ofr20211118.pdf
• Lahars are "the greatest threats that Mount Rainier poses to people and property downstream"; Rainier is "the most threatening volcano in the Cascades"; summit elevation 4,392 metres (USGS, Hazards at Mount Rainier)
https://www.usgs.gov/volcanoes/mount-rainier/science/hazards
• Lahars are "a mixture of mud and rock debris that looks and behaves like flowing concrete"; many debris flows "cannot be predicted and may even occur independently of a volcanic eruption" (USGS FAQ, What is the greatest hazard presented by Mount Rainier?)
https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/what-greatest-hazard-presented-mount-rainier
• Mount Rainier's 25 glaciers cover 92 square kilometres (35 square miles) (USGS, Glaciers Help to Shape Mount Rainier)
https://www.usgs.gov/volcanoes/mount-rainier/science/glaciers-help-shape-mount-rainier
• Glacier area of about 36 square miles and a volume of about 1 cubic mile (C.L. Driedger, USGS Fact Sheet, Glaciers on Mount Rainier)
https://wa.water.usgs.gov/pubs/fs/fs_rainier.html
• Holocene, or post-glacial, eruptions of Mount Rainier; no tephra or other deposit has been found to confirm the reported 1894 eruption (USGS)
https://www.usgs.gov/volcanoes/mount-rainier/science/holocene-or-post-glacial-eruptions-mount-rainier
• Lahar hazard information and arrival-time map for the Puyallup and Nisqually river valleys (USGS)
https://www.usgs.gov/media/images/mount-rainier-volcano-hazards-and-lahar-arrival-times-puyallup-and-nisqually-river
The historical lahars: Osceola and Electron
• Significant lahars at Mount Rainier: the Osceola Mudflow about 5,600 years ago began as an avalanche of 2 to 3 cubic kilometres of mainly hydrothermally altered rock from the summit and northeast slope, leaving a horseshoe-shaped crater 1.8 km (1 mile) wide open to the northeast; it occurred during a period of eruptions; deposits cover about 550 square kilometres (212 square miles), reached Puget Sound near Auburn and extend at least to Kent and to Commencement Bay, now the Port of Tacoma; Orting, Buckley, Sumner, Puyallup, Enumclaw and Auburn are "wholly or partly located on top of" its deposits; the Electron Mudflow of about 500 years ago originated on the west flank near Sunset Amphitheater; the Round Pass Mudflow (about 2,500 years ago) and National Lahar (about 2,200 years ago) (USGS)
https://www.usgs.gov/volcanoes/mount-rainier/science/significant-lahars-mount-rainier
• The Osceola Mudflow total flow volume of about 3.8 cubic kilometres, travelling more than 120 km (about 75 miles) north and west, filling White River valleys to depths of more than 100 metres (Vallance & Scott, USGS, The Osceola Mudflow from Mount Rainier)
https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70020065
• Mount Rainier lahar hazards: the Osceola Mudflow was "at least ten times larger than any other known lahar" from Mount Rainier; Electron deposits more than 100 feet thick at the community of Electron and as much as 20 feet at Orting; "many geologists agree that Mount Rainier is the most dangerous volcano in the Cascade Range"; Rainier's eleven rivers lead to six lowland valleys with a combined regional population of more than 2.5 million (a valley-system total, NOT a hazard-zone population); Hazus-MH economic loss modelling for a noncohesive Electron-like lahar down the Puyallup, about $12 billion using default census-tract inventory, refined to about $6 billion using county assessor parcel data (Cakir & Walsh, Washington DNR / Washington Geological Survey Information Circular 113, May 2012)
https://dnr.wa.gov/publications/ger_ic113_mt_rainier_lahar_hazards.pdf
• Forest-floor burial in 1507 by the largest Mount Rainier lahar of the past millennium: tree-ring crossdating of 21 trees killed by the Electron Mudflow, with a radiocarbon wiggle-match bracketing the event between 1477 and 1522 CE at 99.7 percent confidence, dating it to late summer 1507 (Geology, v.54, 2026)
https://pubs.geoscienceworld.org/gsa/geology/article-abstract/54/2/189/722051/Forest-floor-burial-in-1507-by-the-largest-Mount
• History of landslides and debris flows at Mount Rainier: the 1947 Kautz Creek debris flows, the 1963 Little Tahoma Peak rockfall, and the National Lahar traced 95 km (59 miles) to Puget Sound (USGS)
https://wa.water.usgs.gov/pubs/fs/fs_landslide.html
• Washington DNR overview of volcanoes and lahars in Washington State
https://dnr.wa.gov/washington-geological-survey/geologic-hazards-and-environment/volcanoes-and-lahars
• Lahars as a volcanic process (National Park Service)
https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/volcanic-processes-lahars.htm
Population, assets and infrastructure in the lahar hazard zone
• More than 90,000 people "live in areas that could be impacted by a future large lahar"; non-eruptive west-flank lahars could reach nearby small towns within 5 to 10 minutes and larger communities within 60 minutes; the lahar detection system "reduces — but does not eliminate — risk" (USGS, The Mount Rainier Lahar Detection System, May 15, 2024)
https://www.usgs.gov/media/videos/mount-rainier-lahar-detection-system-risk-mitigation-unlikely-potentially-catastrophic
• Community exposure to lahar hazards from Mount Rainier: 78,049 residents inside an Electron-based hazard zone across King, Lewis, Pierce and Thurston counties (based on circa-2000 census data); 59,678 employees at 3,890 businesses generating $16 billion in annual sales; tax parcels worth $8.8 billion; 63 schools, 54 of them in Pierce County; 30 child day-care facilities and 19 adult residential care facilities; 11 percent of residents over age 65 and 21 percent outside incorporated cities or towns; Mount Rainier National Park and the Puyallup Fairgrounds inside the zone; key road and rail corridors and the Port of Tacoma; at least 60 lahars of various sizes over the past 10,000 years; roughly a 1-in-10 chance of a lahar reaching developed areas of the Puget Sound lowland in an average lifespan; more than 23,000 killed at Armero in 1985; 20th-century lahars worldwide caused more than 30,000 deaths, made 93,000 homeless and affected 1,000,000 people (Wood & Soulard, USGS Scientific Investigations Report 2009-5211)
https://pubs.usgs.gov/sir/2009/5211/sir2009-5211.pdf
• Variations in population exposure and sensitivity to lahar hazards from Mount Rainier (Wood & Soulard, Journal of Volcanology and Geothermal Research 188)
https://www.usgs.gov/publications/variations-population-exposure-and-sensitivity-lahar-hazards-mount-rainier-washington
• Pierce County Hazard Identification and Risk Assessment 2020, volcano chapter: "there were nine large lahars in last 5,600 years, eight of which were associated with eruptions"; "the most likely scenario is a large lahar occurring during unrest/eruption"; for non-eruptive events "there may not be any detectable precursors to the event (minutes to tens of minutes of warning)"; a lahar "has the potential to cause the highest number of fatalities and casualties of any hazard treated in this risk assessment"; "most of Puyallup, almost all of Sumner and all of Orting could be destroyed totally"; "having a warning system in place does not mean that everyone will be able to evacuate the valley bottoms in time... there could be many fatalities"; first-responder-assisted evacuation in Orting "will be out of the question"; arrival times of about 10 minutes to the Nisqually park entrance, about 20 minutes to Ashford and about 60 minutes to Orting; Osceola-scale flows occur "far less frequently than once every 1,000 years"; a 3 percent chance of 1 mm of ash reaching Puyallup even from a repeat of Rainier's largest known eruption, modelled with wind fields from 1,000 random days; over 150,000 people work and reside on the deposits of prehistoric and historic debris flows; the Armero flow was relatively small compared with some Rainier lahars, with mud depths of 6.5 to 16 feet, and about 5,000 injured; I-5, passenger and freight rail, and the Port of Tacoma as the county's economic and industrial base
https://www.piercecountywa.gov/DocumentCenter/View/115034/2020-PC-Volcano-Final

The detection system, evacuation research and the Orting bridge
• Monitoring lahars at Mount Rainier: the lahar detection system became operational in 1998 and is still in operation; USGS maintains the detection component with assistance from Pierce County Department of Emergency Management, while notification is operated by Pierce County, South Sound 911 and Washington State Emergency Management Division, with the Pacific Northwest Seismic Network, the Washington Military Department and Mount Rainier National Park as partners; modelled arrival times of about 5 minutes to residential areas inside the park and 15 to 60 minutes outside it (USGS)
https://www.usgs.gov/volcanoes/mount-rainier/science/monitoring-lahars-mount-rainier
• Lahar detection system upgraded for Mount Rainier: the original system used buried acoustic flow monitors and tripwires, "designed to have low bandwidth and low power requirements due to the limitations of 1990s-era technology, and that meant that data was only transmitted every two minutes," producing "at least four minutes of delay between when the lahar had gone past and when the system said, 'hey, a lahar has gone past'"; 14 new sites installed between 2017 and spring 2021 with up to 20 more planned, 12 of them proposed inside the national park, heading toward more than 40 real-time monitoring stations; new broadband seismometers, GPS receivers, web cameras and infrasound sensors, "literally just a fancy microphone that's tuned to record things that are below our normal range of hearing"; laser range finders being tested to replace tripwires; coverage expanded from the Carbon and Puyallup valleys to the Tahoma Creek and Nisqually drainage (Seismological Society of America)
https://www.seismosoc.org/news/lahar-detection-system-upgraded-for-mount-rainier/
• USGS installs new sensors to detect and warn communities of lahars from Mount Rainier (KOMO News)
https://komonews.com/news/local/mount-rainier-seismic-activity-new-sensors-installed-us-geological-survey-lahar-detection-early-alert-warning-system-white-river-communities-seismology-science
• Influence of modeling assumptions on pedestrian evacuation success for non-eruptive lahar hazards at Mount Rainier: 736 modelled scenarios varying lahar source, safety destination, pedestrian travel speed and departure delay; "some communities may have substantial loss of life in tens of minutes after lahar initiation, whereas other communities may be managing large-scale evacuations over several hours"; populations at stake include tens of thousands of residents and employees, thousands of primary and secondary school students, and hundreds of individuals in long-term residential care (Wood & Peters, International Journal of Disaster Risk Reduction, 2026, DOI 10.1016/j.ijdrr.2026.106132)
