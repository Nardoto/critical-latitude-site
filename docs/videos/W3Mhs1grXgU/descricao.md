Long Valley Caldera - California's supervolcano - is cooling down. The new imaging that proved it does not make the place safe. It moves the danger.

In April 2006, three members of the Mammoth Mountain ski patrol died on a volcano that did not erupt. Carbon dioxide, odorless and heavier than air, had filled a cavity under the snowpack. That is what Long Valley actually does. Seven hundred and sixty thousand years ago this spot erupted roughly 600 cubic kilometers of magma and collapsed into a basin 20 miles by 11 in eastern California. Today that basin holds a highway, a ski resort, and about 7,000 year-round residents in the town of Mammoth Lakes.

Here is what the data says. The 2023 Science Advances study out of Caltech turned 100 kilometers of fiber optic cable into the equivalent of 10,000 seismometers and imaged a solid crystallized lid sealing the magma chamber from above. Uplift peaked around 2022 and is reversing. Only six earthquakes of magnitude 1.0 or larger were located inside the caldera in all of 2025. The U.S.G.S. alert level is normal, and the agency reports no evidence a catastrophic eruption is brewing. The real eruption risk moved north to the Mono-Inyo Craters chain, roughly one in a few hundred in any given year. Meanwhile 170 acres of forest are dead from volcanic carbon dioxide. This is not a prediction that an eruption is imminent.
