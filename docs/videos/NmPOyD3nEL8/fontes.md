Sources — Mount St. Helens Is Shaking Again — Scientists Watch 5 Signs, And Every One Says No
Educational explainer of what the monitoring instruments at Mount St. Helens currently show. Not a prediction that an eruption is imminent.
Live pages — check these yourself
USGS Volcano Updates for Mount St. Helens. The alert level and aviation color code, updated continuously. NORMAL / GREEN as of recording.
https://www.usgs.gov/volcanoes/mount-st.-helens/volcano-updates
Pacific Northwest Seismic Network, Mount St. Helens. Every located earthquake with magnitude, depth and time. Free, no account required. This is the catalog shown on screen, including the magnitude minus 0.3 event.
https://pnsn.org/volcanoes/mount-st-helens
USGS Volcano Notification Service (HANS). Where the Cascades Volcano Observatory posts status updates and information statements.
https://volcanoes.usgs.gov/hans-public/
Baseline seismicity and the recharge mechanism
Earthquake Monitoring at Mount St. Helens. Source for the long-term average of about 22 located events per month, the depth thresholds separating deep magma arrival from shallow pre-eruptive seismicity, the 1987 to 2004 deep swarms attributed to recharge, and the line "high rates of seismicity, interpreted as recharge, have been observed in the past at Mount St. Helens and at other volcanoes, and can continue for many years without an eruption." Also the source for the claim that this volcano has the best seismic monitoring network in the Cascade Range.
https://www.usgs.gov/volcanoes/mount-st.-helens/science/earthquake-monitoring-mount-st-helens
Mount St. Helens, main USGS page. Source for "the most active volcano in the Cascade Range, and it is the most likely of the contiguous U.S. volcanoes to erupt in the future," and the Very High threat tier.
https://www.usgs.gov/volcanoes/mount-st.-helens
Volcanic gas
Volcanic Gas Monitoring at Mount St. Helens. Source for carbon dioxide separating from magma deeper than other gases, for "increased rates of SO2 measured before several dome extrusions were signs that magma was moving at a more rapid rate toward the crater vent," and for the peak carbon dioxide emissions of over 22,000 tons per day between July 1980 and August 1981.
https://www.usgs.gov/volcanoes/mount-st.-helens/science/volcanic-gas-monitoring-mount-st-helens
Previous swarms that went nowhere
Small magnitude earthquake swarm continues at Mount St. Helens, USGS, May 5 2016. Source for the 2016 swarm at depths of 2 to 7 kilometers, more than 130 located events in eight weeks, largest magnitude 1.3, a rate approaching 40 located events per week, and the statement "no anomalous gases, increases in ground inflation or shallow seismicity have been detected with this swarm, and there are no signs of an imminent eruption." Also the comparison to the 2013 and 2014 swarms.
https://www.usgs.gov/news/small-magnitude-earthquake-swarm-continues-mount-st-helens
Mount St. Helens seismicity elevated but within the range of background levels, Cascades Volcano Observatory. Source for the roughly 350 earthquakes between February 1 and June 17 2024, over 95 percent below magnitude 1.0, median depth about 3.5 miles below sea level, and "there have been no significant changes in other monitoring parameters (ground deformation, volcanic gas or thermal emissions)."
https://www.usgs.gov/observatories/cvo/news/mount-st-helens-seismicity-elevated-within-range-background-levels-february
Uptick in earthquake activity at Mount St. Helens remains within background levels, Cascades Volcano Observatory, 2023.
https://www.usgs.gov/observatories/cvo/news/uptick-earthquake-activity-mount-st-helens-remains-within-background-levels
Seattle Times coverage of the 2024 swarm. Source for the Weston Thelen quote: "thus far we haven't seen a change in the shape of the volcano, either inflating or deflating, and we haven't seen any changes in gas output," and his expectation that a future eruption would most likely build a dome. This quote lives in press coverage of a USGS scientist, not in a USGS publication.
https://www.seattletimes.com/seattle-news/northwest/mount-st-helens-shakes-120-times-within-a-week-as-volcano-recharges-scientists-say/
The Hill coverage of the same statements.
https://thehill.com/changing-america/resilience/natural-disasters/4730536-mount-st-helens-recharging-amid-increase-in-earthquake-activity-usgs/
The alert level system
USGS Volcano Hazards Program, Alert Level System. Source for the four ground alert levels: NORMAL, ADVISORY, WATCH and WARNING, with WATCH defined as "an eruption is likely, or an eruption is occurring with limited hazards."
https://www.usgs.gov/programs/VHP/alert-level-system
Volcanic alert-levels characterize conditions at U.S. volcanoes. Source for the two parallel ladders, and for the aviation color code being about ash reaching aircraft rather than hazards on the ground.
https://www.usgs.gov/programs/VHP/volcanic-alert-levels-characterize-conditions-us-volcanoes
The 1980 eruption
1980 Cataclysmic Eruption, USGS. Source for the March 16 1980 earthquakes as the first sign of activity, the March 27 steam eruption as the first in over 100 years, the north flank expanding at about 2 meters or 6.5 feet per day to a total displacement of about 140 meters or 450 feet, the magnitude 5.1 at 8:32 a.m. on May 18, the debris avalanche volume of about 2.5 cubic kilometers, and the lateral blast affecting 600 square kilometers or 230 square miles.
https://www.usgs.gov/volcanoes/mount-st.-helens/science/1980-cataclysmic-eruption
USGS FAQ, summit elevation before and after. Source for 9,677 feet before and 8,363 feet after, a loss of 1,314 feet. Note that the main eruption page instead describes the upper 300 meters being removed; the FAQ figures are used here because that page exists to answer this specific question.
https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/how-high-was-mount-st-helens-may-18-1980-eruption-how-high-was-it-after
Mount St. Helens 1980 eruption changed the future of volcanology, USGS. Source for the 57 deaths, and for David Johnston, the USGS volcanologist at the observation post north of the mountain whose name the Cascades Volcano Observatory carries.
https://www.usgs.gov/news/featured-story/mount-st-helens-1980-eruption-changed-future-volcanology
Threat ranking
2018 Update to the U.S. Geological Survey National Volcanic Threat Assessment, Scientific Investigations Report 2018-5140. Source for Mount St. Helens ranking second at 235, Mount Rainier third at 203, and Yellowstone twenty-first at 115.
https://pubs.usgs.gov/sir/2018/5140/
Lahar and mudflow hazard maps
Volcanic Hazards at Mount St. Helens, USGS. The hazard map showing which drainages carry lahars off this mountain.
https://www.usgs.gov/volcanoes/mount-st.-helens/science/volcanic-hazards-mount-st-helens
Washington Department of Natural Resources, Volcanoes and Lahars. The state hazard-mapping page for the Toutle and Cowlitz drainages.
https://dnr.wa.gov/washington-geological-survey/geologic-hazards-and-environment/volcanoes-and-lahars
Washington Geological Survey volcano hazards brochure (PDF). Contains the lahar hazard map shown on screen and the figures for lahar travel distance and speed.
https://dnr.wa.gov/sites/default/files/2025-03/ger_volcano_hazards_brochure.pdf
