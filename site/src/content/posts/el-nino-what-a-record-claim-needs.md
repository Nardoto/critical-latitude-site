There's a rectangle of ocean near the equator, roughly between Hawaii and South America, that can change the odds of winter weather far beyond the Pacific. The number attached to it is a temperature difference, not the temperature of the water itself.

In the July 2026 material I examined, the Niño 3.4 anomaly was about 1.2 degrees Celsius above normal. A forecast assembled from 14 seasonal systems and 667 ensemble members had a median peak of 3.6 degrees. Those are different statements: one describes conditions being observed, the other an outcome the models projected.

Zeke Hausfather's July 13 analysis in *The Climate Brink*, *The Strongest El Nino Ever*, put the probability of exceeding the historical record at about 91 percent. The July 9 NOAA Climate Prediction Center discussion assigned an 81 percent chance of a very strong event during October–December and a 97 percent chance of persistence into early spring.

Keep the dates attached to those probabilities. They describe that forecast issue, not a live bulletin or an observed record already broken.

I want to give the conclusion before the mechanism: an exceptional Pacific temperature anomaly does not guarantee an exceptional disaster on your street. It changes the odds of particular conditions in particular regions. Understanding the ocean is the first step. Translating it into local impacts requires another set of judgments.

## The wind holds warm water in the west

Under normal conditions, equatorial trade winds push surface water west across the Pacific. Warm water accumulates around Indonesia, Papua New Guinea and northern Australia. Meanwhile, water moving away from the eastern Pacific is replaced from below.

That upwelling brings relatively cold, nutrient-rich water toward the surface off Peru and Ecuador. The nutrients support plankton and the anchoveta fishery. The arrangement connects a wind pattern over an ocean with food and livelihoods along its edge.

When the trade winds weaken, the warm-water distribution changes. A downwelling Kelvin wave can carry the disturbance eastward below the surface. It is not a breaking wave that someone would recognize from a boat. It changes the depth of the thermocline, the boundary between the warmer upper ocean and colder water below.

Deepen the thermocline in the east and upwelling no longer brings the same cold water to the surface. The eastern and central Pacific warm. NOAA's explanations of Kelvin waves and the tropical Pacific thermocline describe this mechanism.

Then the atmosphere responds. Convection and thunderstorms shift toward the warmer water. That changes the circulation, which can weaken the trade winds further and reinforce the ocean change.

The ocean and atmosphere are interacting, not simply taking turns. That is why a Niño 3.4 reading belongs beside observations of winds, subsurface heat and convection. A thermometer alone does not describe the whole coupled system.

## A warmer Pacific can discourage Atlantic hurricanes

A hurricane needs its circulation to remain organized vertically. Strong differences in wind speed or direction between lower and upper levels can push that structure apart. This is vertical wind shear.

El Niño tends to increase shear over the tropical Atlantic and Caribbean. NOAA's Atlantic Oceanographic and Meteorological Laboratory explains the connection between Pacific conditions and reduced Atlantic hurricane activity, alongside a tendency toward increased activity in the eastern Pacific.

The NOAA Atlantic outlook discussed in the July material gave a 55 percent chance of a below-normal season, a 35 percent chance of near-normal activity and a 10 percent chance of above-normal activity. Its ranges were 8–14 named storms, 3–6 hurricanes and 1–3 major hurricanes. These were seasonal probabilities and ranges.

A below-normal season still contains storms. The 1992 Atlantic season was below average, and it included Hurricane Andrew. A basin-wide count cannot tell a household whether the particular storm that forms will reach its town.

The same Pacific convection changes also affect the jet stream. In a typical El Niño winter, the subtropical jet strengthens and the storm track shifts south across the United States. NOAA's winter-impact guidance associates that pattern with wetter conditions across much of the southern tier and warmer conditions across much of the north. Those are tendencies across events, not instructions that every individual storm must follow.

## The historical comparison needs an index and a method

Niño 3.4 is an average sea-surface temperature anomaly in a defined equatorial Pacific region. A sustained anomaly around half a degree above normal is part of the El Niño definition, alongside the atmospheric response. Events at or above roughly 2 degrees are commonly described as very strong.

Hausfather's comparison placed the strongest historical events around 2.69–2.75 degrees: the 1982–83 event, the reconstructed 1877 event, and the 1997–98 and 2015–16 events. A projected peak of 3.6 degrees would be a large departure from that comparison group.

But “155 years” does not mean 155 years of identical instruments and coverage. The nineteenth-century part involves reconstructed conditions. It must be distinguished from the better observed modern record. A historical comparison can be useful without pretending all its values were measured in the same way.

The baseline also changes the answer. As the tropical oceans warm, an absolute anomaly can combine broad background warming with the Pacific pattern itself. NOAA's Relative Oceanic Niño Index accounts for temperature change across the wider tropical oceans.

In the analysis discussed here, the probability of a record fell from about 91 percent to about 77 percent when assessed with the relative measure. That did not remove the prospect of an unusually strong event. It showed that the size of the record claim depends on what, exactly, is being compared.

## What happened in 1997–98 was not uniform

California experienced a very wet 1997–98 season, with roughly double normal rainfall in many places. The USGS's 1999 fact sheet, *El Niño Sea-Level Rise Wreaks Havoc in California's San Francisco Bay Region*, reports about $850 million in statewide flood and storm damage.

Landslides in the ten-county San Francisco Bay region caused more than $150 million in losses over the winter and following spring. Those figures belong to that historical episode and its reporting, rather than today's prices.

Stanley Changnon's *Impacts of 1997–98 El Niño-Generated Weather in the United States*, published in the *Bulletin of the American Meteorological Society* in 1999, records 189 deaths nationally. Southern tornadoes were a substantial part of the toll. The event was not only a California rain story.

Elsewhere, the consequences followed other pathways. Suppressed upwelling affected Peru's fishery. Drought risk affected agricultural regions, while prolonged warmth stressed coral reefs. These connections involve different processes and response times, which is why a single national weather map cannot contain the whole event.

Nor does a stronger ocean anomaly multiply damage by the same proportion. Where storms travel, what is exposed and how people are prepared all affect the result. The strong 2015–16 El Niño did not deliver the Southern California rainfall many people expected. The physical connection is real, but one event can depart from its familiar regional pattern.

## What people asked

**How can the record extend beyond 1950?** Readers questioned the length and completeness of the comparison. Earlier El Niño conditions can be reconstructed from historical evidence and climate proxies. That extends the usable record, but it does not make nineteenth-century estimates equivalent to modern measurements. The reconstruction, index and baseline need to stay visible in the claim.

**Why do people's memories include blizzards where the map suggests warmth?** Readers recalled snow in Colorado, Phoenix and Michigan, alongside flooding and tornadoes elsewhere. A seasonal tendency does not rule out an individual cold spell or snowstorm. Location also matters: the boundary between broad regional patterns is not a line that storms agree to respect.

I also want to retain the uncertainty in the models. Seasonal ENSO forecasts generally improve after the Northern Hemisphere spring predictability barrier. That helps explain the July confidence, but 667 simulations are not 667 independent confirmations of the future.

Tim Stockdale's ECMWF discussion of forecast confidence describes overconfidence in individual systems and past failures. Models can share assumptions and errors. A 3.6-degree outcome would still have to be observed.

The next useful information is the next measurement: trade winds, subsurface warmth and the updated CPC diagnostic discussion. A Pacific forecast can justify paying attention to regional hazards. It cannot replace the local forecast when a particular storm develops.
