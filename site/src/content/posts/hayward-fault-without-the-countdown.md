There's a curb over the Hayward Fault that somebody poured straight. It isn't straight now. The two sides of the ground move past each other, and the concrete has to accommodate that movement. The rate is about a fifth of an inch, or 5 millimeters, each year. Slow enough to miss on an ordinary walk. Persistent enough to bend a street.

Farther north, the same fault runs directly underneath California Memorial Stadium at the University of California, Berkeley. The stadium has been retrofitted around that problem. The USGS counts more than 300 buildings and structures on the fault trace. This is a moving geological boundary with a city built across it.

I went through the USGS paperwork because the word attached to this fault everywhere is “overdue.” The last large earthquake was on October 21, 1868, about 158 years before this account. Put that beside an average recurrence interval of about 150 years and the subtraction seems straightforward.

But an average needs the rest of its record. Before attaching a deadline to that number, we need to look at what happened in 1868, what the shallow fault is doing now, and what the deeper fault has been doing while the curb slowly bends.

## The creeping part is only part of the fault

The 1868 earthquake had an estimated magnitude of 6.8. Shaking lasted more than 40 seconds. About 30 people died, and the surface rupture extended roughly 20 miles from Warm Springs, now part of Fremont, north to San Leandro. The two sides moved about 6 feet past each other on average.

Hayward had roughly 500 residents. Almost every building was destroyed or badly damaged. Until 1906, this was the event people meant by the great San Francisco earthquake. Those details come from Brocher, Boatwright, Lienkaemper, Prentice, Schwartz and Bundock's 2018 USGS fact sheet, *The Hayward Fault — Is it due for a repeat of the powerful 1868 earthquake?*

The creep sounds reassuring at first. If the fault keeps slipping a little, perhaps it never needs to slip a lot. The problem is depth. The creeping is concentrated in the upper 3 miles, about 5 kilometers. Below that, locked sections continue accumulating strain.

Over the long term, creep and small earthquakes account for about one third of the movement the fault must accommodate. They do not account for the other two thirds. A bent curb is evidence of shallow motion, not a gauge showing that the whole fault has safely emptied its stored strain. The locked section remains part of the same system.

## How a trench becomes an earthquake history

The USGS identifies 12 major southern Hayward earthquakes over roughly 1,900 years. The six most recent dates listed are 1134, 1317, 1475, 1629, 1725 and 1868. These are geological estimates. They were not all read from dated eyewitness accounts.

Geologists dig across the fault and examine the exposed layers. A rupture cuts or displaces those layers. Organic material below and above the break, such as charcoal or a seed, can be radiocarbon dated. The earthquake falls between the ages of the material bracketing it. Repeating that work builds a history with uncertainty around the dates.

Subtract those six listed years and the intervals are 183, 158, 154, 96 and 143 years. Their mean is about 147 years, close to the rounded 150-year figure. The spread is the point. Removing the longest and shortest intervals would remove evidence about how irregularly the fault behaves.

The broader HayWired fact sheet describes roughly one large earthquake every 100 to 220 years, with an average interval around 150 years plus or minus 60. That is a description of past variability. It is not a permitted window outside which an earthquake cannot happen. Nor is the longest interval in the six-event subset a maximum waiting time for the future.

## What the probabilities actually cover

The often repeated 72 percent figure covers a magnitude 6.7 or greater earthquake somewhere in the entire San Francisco Bay region. It adds together the regional hazard across multiple faults. It is not a 72 percent probability assigned to Hayward alone.

The roughly one-in-three figure associated with Hayward comes from the USGS *Earthquake Outlook for the San Francisco Bay Region 2014–2043*, published in 2016. Keep that window attached to it. It is not a fresh 30-year forecast beginning whenever someone reads this article, and one third simply means about 33 percent within the stated assessment.

The other sentence in Brocher and colleagues' fact sheet is less comfortable: “a powerful, damaging earthquake can be expected at any time.” That assessment and the irregular recurrence record fit together. A fault can present a serious hazard without having missed an appointment.

My concern with “overdue” is the calendar it puts in your head. It suggests that there was a safe period before the deadline and a different kind of certainty afterward. The evidence does not provide either. Small earthquakes happen frequently, but no demonstrated signal lets us identify which particular small event will precede a large one. Preparing depends on the established hazard, not on finding a countdown in the latest tremor.

## What changes when the ground motion is modeled

In 2018, the USGS published HayWired, a scenario built around a hypothetical magnitude 7.0 earthquake beginning beneath Oakland. It modeled about 52 miles of rupture, 800 deaths, 18,000 injuries from structural damage, and more than $82 billion in property and direct business interruption losses, expressed in 2016 dollars. These are scenario results, not an exact toll waiting to occur.

The details also challenge the idea that distance alone decides who shakes hardest. Artie Rodgers and colleagues at Lawrence Livermore and Lawrence Berkeley modeled ground motion at higher frequencies than earlier simulations. LLNL's account of their work in *Geophysical Research Letters* explains the improvement from roughly 0.5–1 hertz to 4–5 hertz.

That brings the faster vibrations relevant to smaller buildings into better focus. In the modeled rupture, soft sedimentary rock beneath the East Bay hills amplified shaking. Inland communities such as Orinda and Moraga could experience stronger shaking than their position away from the bay might suggest. That result is tied to the modeled earthquake and geology, not a ranking of every house.

The useful responses are already physical: retrofitted structures, ShakeAlert, and pipelines designed to move. Alameda County Water District's Alvarado-Niles work replaces rigid pipe across the fault with a crossing intended to accommodate displacement. Those choices change consequences before anyone knows the earthquake's date.

## What people asked

**Does averaging the dates show that the fault is late?** Several readers tried that calculation. The average describes the center of an uneven record; it does not set the next event's date. Discarding extreme intervals makes the record look more regular than it is.

**Does a longer wait guarantee a larger earthquake?** Readers also connected additional years with additional strain. Loading matters, but elapsed time alone does not determine how much fault will rupture or how much it will slip. These sources do not establish a rule that twice the wait produces twice the displacement.

**Were there really no people here to remember earlier earthquakes?** There were. My wording about nobody being there was wrong. Indigenous communities lived in the region, and a geological chronology should not erase them or dismiss oral histories. The narrower point is that the listed numerical dates were reconstructed from physical evidence in trenches.

That is the standard I want to keep: correct the wording when it outruns the evidence. The curb is moving, the deeper fault is accumulating strain, and the region has buildings and utilities worth strengthening. None of those facts needs an invented deadline to matter.
