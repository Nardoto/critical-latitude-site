A storm surge raises the water and then recedes. Sea-level rise changes the level from which the next storm starts. That is the difference I want to keep in view when we talk about Thwaites Glacier.

Thwaites drains into the Amundsen Sea in West Antarctica. Its basin covers about 192,000 square kilometers, roughly 74,000 square miles, and its ice front spans about 120 kilometers. The International Thwaites Glacier Collaboration puts its contribution at around 4 percent of global sea-level rise.

The figures in this account describe ice loss of roughly 50 billion tons a year beyond replacement by snowfall. If the whole glacier were lost, its contribution to global mean sea level would be about 65 centimeters, or 25 inches. That is a measure of the ice it contains, not a forecast that this rise is about to arrive.

The larger West Antarctic Ice Sheet contains enough ice for roughly 3.3 meters of global mean rise. Full loss is discussed on timescales of centuries to millennia, not as a scheduled event. These quantities should never become interchangeable.

I find the “doomsday” nickname unhelpful because it can obscure both the measurements and the uncertainties. The useful story is underneath the ice: warm seawater, a bed that deepens inland, fractures, and a floating shelf that resists some of the glacier's motion.

## The ground gets deeper as the ice retreats

A glacier's grounding line marks the transition between ice resting on the bed and ice floating on seawater. At Thwaites, much of the bed lies below sea level and deepens inland. That is called a retrograde slope.

The geometry can favor further retreat. As the grounding line moves into deeper water, thicker ice is involved at the transition to flotation, and the glacier can discharge more ice. Under suitable conditions, retreat reinforces itself. Scientists call this marine ice sheet instability.

The shape of the bed is part of the explanation, not a stopwatch. The glacier's behavior also depends on ocean conditions, friction and the forces exerted by the floating ice in front of it. Knowing that an instability mechanism exists is different from knowing its exact rate.

Warmth reaches Thwaites from below. Relatively warm, salty circumpolar deep water can move along the seafloor into the cavity beneath the glacier's floating ice. Cold water at the surface does not tell us the temperature at that depth.

This is why researchers wanted measurements inside the cavity. Looking down from a satellite can reveal changes in ice position and motion. It cannot directly measure every part of the ocean–ice contact. The British Antarctic Survey's account of the International Thwaites Glacier Collaboration describes a joint U.S.–U.K. program built to close those gaps with instruments on and under the glacier.

## Icefin found an uneven underside

Two papers published in *Nature* in 2023, by Schmidt and colleagues and Davis and colleagues, described observations near the grounding line. Researchers drilled a borehole about 600 meters through the ice and lowered instruments, including the Icefin robot, into the ocean cavity.

The flat underside was melting more slowly than models had anticipated. Rates discussed in the measurements were roughly 6 to 18 feet a year. Taken alone, that finding would sound reassuring.

But the underside was not a flat ceiling. Icefin observed terraces and crevasses, with much faster melting on steep surfaces. Warm water could work into those features while meltwater near flatter surfaces helped limit heat transfer there.

That distinction changes what an average melt rate can tell us. A modest average does not mean the ice is being thinned evenly, and slower melting across one surface does not mean that its fractures are stable.

The instrument record also showed water becoming warmer and saltier over the observation period. Heat was available in the cavity even where the measured melting did not follow the simple expectation of a uniformly eroding ceiling.

What I take from those papers is the value of seeing the geometry directly. The ocean is interacting with steps and cracks. Models need to represent that interaction if they are going to connect local melting with the glacier's larger response.

## The floating shelf acts as a brake

The Thwaites Eastern Ice Shelf is already floating. Its importance is the resistance it provides to ice behind it, partly through contact with an underwater pinning point. Remove some of that resistance and the grounded glacier can accelerate.

That is different from saying the shelf's own melting will add its full volume to sea level. Floating ice already displaces seawater. The larger concern is what happens to grounded ice when the shelf can no longer provide the same restraint.

Erin Pettit's December 2021 presentation at the American Geophysical Union meeting drew attention to spreading fractures and the possibility of relatively rapid shelf breakup. It was a warning about a developing process, not a reliable calendar for the disappearance of the glacier.

The 2026 reporting collected here describes shelf speed increasing roughly threefold between January 2020 and January 2026, to just over 2,000 meters a year. Fractures around the pinning point and grounding line are evidence of change. They do not establish the date of a complete breakup.

After any further loss of shelf restraint, the quantity researchers need is the glacier's measured response: how much its motion changes, and how quickly. That is a stronger basis for refining projections than treating every new crack as confirmation of the most dramatic possible outcome.

## The fastest collapse hypothesis did not hold up

Marine ice cliff instability is a separate proposed mechanism from marine ice sheet instability. It asks whether tall ice cliffs exposed after shelf loss could fail under their own weight, repeatedly exposing another unstable cliff behind them.

Mathieu Morlighem and colleagues tested this in *Science Advances* in 2024 using three ice-sheet models. Their experiments did not support the rapid, self-sustaining cliff-collapse scenario for Thwaites under the conditions examined.

The study discussed cliffs around 135 meters high and a more gradual response than the fastest-collapse hypothesis assumed. Dartmouth's account also preserves the author's warning: testing one mechanism does not establish that Thwaites is stable against every other process.

Warm ocean water, grounding-line retreat and changing shelf restraint remain relevant. A narrower result about cliff failure cannot cancel those observations. It also should not be dismissed simply because it reduces one particularly alarming projection.

The coastal consequences have another complication: sea level does not rise equally everywhere. A large ice sheet attracts nearby seawater gravitationally. As it loses mass, that attraction weakens and water redistributes. NASA calls the resulting geographic pattern a sea-level fingerprint.

In NASA's example of complete Amundsen Sea sector loss, a global average rise of about 4 feet corresponds to an additional 14–15 inches above that average along the U.S. East Coast. That is a scenario for a larger Antarctic sector, not the contribution of Thwaites alone.

## What people asked

**Why would melting ice raise sea level if an ice cube does not overflow a glass?** Several readers made this comparison. It captures the basic behavior of already floating ice. Much of Thwaites rests on the bed, however. Its loss adds to the ocean, and losing the floating shelf can reduce the resistance holding that grounded ice back.

**Could a changing shoreline reflect sinking land instead?** Readers reported both increasing flooding and places where they had noticed little change. Local relative sea level combines ocean change with land movement. A personal observation is useful context, but it does not isolate the cause or measure a global trend.

**Does Thwaites tell us what will happen to currents and climate?** Readers asked about the Southern Ocean and Atlantic circulation. Freshwater, heat and circulation belong in that broader research, but the gravitational fingerprint described here does not require a forecast of Atlantic circulation collapse. The cited glacier observations do not settle a global warming-or-cooling outcome from Thwaites alone.

NOAA's 2022 sea-level technical report projects about 10–12 inches of additional rise along the U.S. coastline by 2050. That is a dated, multi-agency projection incorporating several processes; it should not be labeled a Thwaites-only forecast or simply added to another overlapping estimate.

For coastal planning, NOAA's Sea Level Rise Viewer and local flood information bring the question back to the actual place. The glacier's measurements describe a changing source of risk. The shoreline's elevation and infrastructure help determine how that change reaches people.
