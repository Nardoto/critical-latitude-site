A section of Anak Krakatau is missing. Before December 22, 2018, that rock was part of a volcano. After its southwestern flank collapsed, it lay on the seabed and a tsunami was moving toward the coasts of Java and Sumatra. More than 400 people died.

The missing mountainside is where I want to start, because eruption footage cannot show the whole system. A camera on a boat stops at the waterline. The slope continues underneath, and its shape helps determine how well the material above it is supported.

Anak Krakatau means child of Krakatau. It grew inside the caldera left by the 1883 destruction of the older volcano, in Indonesia's Sunda Strait. NASA Earth Observatory's account of the island shows that larger setting: the visible cone occupies part of a much bigger, partly submerged volcanic structure.

The 2018 tsunami did not require an eruption on the scale of 1883. A large amount of rock entered the sea quickly enough to displace a dangerous amount of water. That is a mechanism we can investigate without assuming every new ash plume will produce the same outcome.

The useful questions are physical. Which part of the slope is moving? Where is new material accumulating? What is supporting it below the water? To answer them, researchers followed the volcano from satellite measurements down to the fallen blocks on the seabed.

## Moving water is different from making an ash cloud

Lower a stone into water and the water level rises. Drop it in and the same displacement happens much faster. My small demonstration splashed more than I expected, but a rock in a container only illustrates the beginning of the process.

A volcanic landslide can fragment as it falls. The seabed slopes away, and the water has room to spread in several directions. The amount and speed of the entering material, the water depth and the surrounding geometry all matter. An ash column's height does not measure any of those directly.

That is why judging the danger only against 1883 is misleading. A volcano can produce ash and hot material without causing a major displacement of seawater. It can also lose part of a flank during a smaller eruption and create a tsunami that reaches people well outside the immediate eruptive area.

The 2018 reconstruction included a small earthquake shortly before the collapse. That signal belongs in the sequence, but the large displacement of water came from the falling mountainside. Calling this an earthquake tsunami would miss the mechanism researchers recovered.

The National Weather Service's tsunami guidance includes volcanic activity and landslides alongside earthquakes as possible sources. For a nearby coast, that means strong ground shaking cannot be the only warning sign people know. The sea can be disturbed even when the coast has not felt a large earthquake.

## What the satellites showed before the collapse

Thomas Walter and colleagues reconstructed the months before failure in *Nature Communications* in 2019. They combined satellite radar, thermal observations and instruments on the ground. The southwestern flank had been moving seaward while eruptions added material to the island.

Repeated radar measurements can reveal a changing position that two ordinary photographs would make difficult to see. I think of a shelf that still looks straight from the front while its distance from the wall has changed. Measuring the gap tells you something your first glance missed.

At a volcano, researchers must separate real movement from other effects in the radar measurements. A repeated pattern provides more information than one unusual observation. Thermal observations added another part of the record by helping identify fresh, hot material and ongoing activity.

The location of that added material matters. Putting more books near the middle of a supported shelf is different from loading an unsupported edge. The analogy does not calculate a failure time, but it explains why the distribution of volcanic deposits matters alongside their volume.

The GFZ account of Walter and colleagues' study emphasizes the retrospective reconstruction. The evidence became clearer once the researchers knew which slope had failed. Identifying changes afterward is different from deciding, beforehand, that they justify evacuating a coast. The study recovered warning signs; it did not establish an exact countdown that was available before the disaster.

## The fallen rock was still there to measure

Eruptions continued after the collapse, laying fresh material over parts of the evidence. Researchers therefore needed a way to see beyond the changing outline of the island.

In August 2019, a marine survey examined the seabed southwest of Anak Krakatau. James Hunt and colleagues reported the results in *Nature Communications* in 2021. Bathymetric mapping and seismic reflection let them investigate both the underwater surface and material beneath it.

Sound travels down through the water and returns from the seabed. Its travel time helps map relief in a place where a satellite photograph mainly shows the ocean surface. Other acoustic measurements help distinguish buried structures and deposits.

The team mapped large blocks from the failed flank in the deep hollow beside the island. Some stood as much as 70 meters, about 230 feet, above the surrounding seabed. That measurement describes the height of landslide blocks above the bottom. It is not a measurement of the tsunami's height.

Comparisons with earlier seabed records connected the underwater deposit with the missing part of the island. The satellite record followed the moving slope before failure. The marine survey found where the material ended up. Those observations address different parts of the event and support the same reconstruction: a mountainside moved into the sea and displaced water toward the coast.

## Regrowth does not restore the old conditions automatically

An island can grow by becoming taller and steeper or by spreading material across a wider area. The same volume added in different places does not create the same slope.

Meredew and colleagues examined Anak Krakatau's development in *Bulletin of Volcanology* in 2026, using historical records, satellite observations and drone data through 2023. Their reconstruction compared growth before and after the collapse.

They found a gentler underwater slope on the southwestern side after the failure. The authors identified that changed geometry as a factor likely to help stability. New growth was taking place on a foundation that the collapse had altered.

They also explored different growth rates. Sustaining the unusually rapid post-collapse rate produced a comparable island shape much sooner than using the slower long-term rate. The authors considered continued growth at that faster rate very unlikely. A calculation about a future shape does not identify when a flank will fail.

There is another date distinction here. Publication in 2026 did not make a reconstruction ending in 2023 a measurement of the island during the September 2026 eruption.

The BMKG monitoring bulletin cited here reported no tsunami-indicating sea-level anomaly in the Sunda Strait through 7:30 a.m. local time on September 6, 2026. That is a dated observation. It says what instruments detected by that time, not what the volcano or sea would do afterward.

## What people asked

**Do smaller, frequent eruptions make another collapse less likely?** Readers connected frequent eruptions with pressure release. Erupting magma can release gas while also adding weight to a slope. An open vent does not establish that the flank is stable. Both the internal activity and the location of new deposits matter.

**Would more water-level sensors solve the warning problem?** Readers suggested concentrating detection at sea. Those instruments are useful, but once they detect a tsunami, water is already moving. Useful warning time depends on the source, the coast and how quickly a message reaches people. Slope monitoring and sea-level monitoring answer different questions.

**Does the smaller island rule out another tsunami, or does regrowth guarantee one?** Both positions appeared in the discussion. The gentler underwater slope is a finding worth keeping. It does not prove zero risk, and regrowth does not prove a scheduled repeat. The available studies describe mechanisms and conditional changes, not a date or inevitable wave size.

For people around the Sunda Strait, Indonesian authorities provide the local instructions. Learn the evacuation route before needing it. Sudden sea-level change or a loud ocean roar can be natural warnings; follow evacuation directions and do not return after the first wave without an official all-clear.

The next photograph will probably show the cone above the sea. The work underneath it is what connects that image to the people across the water.
