At 8:37 a.m. Nepal time on August 26, 2026, seismometers registered a signal near the Chinese border. Automatic processing produced a location and a magnitude: 4.4. The event went into the earthquake catalog.

About seven minutes later, security cameras at the Gyirong Port border crossing recorded the flood arriving. The crossing, customs facilities and the road serving them were devastated. Early reporting counted more than 300 vehicles, 41 bridges and 42 kilometers of road among the losses along the affected system.

The USGS entry for the event no longer classified it as an earthquake after review. Event us7000tbwb was listed as a magnitude 5.2 landslide, at a depth of zero kilometers. The revision came from additional analysis of long-period seismic waves.

I want to keep two questions separate. One is how researchers established that a mountain collapse produced the signal. The other is why detecting that signal did not become a warning for people downstream. A corrected catalog answers the first question. It cannot give a community back the minutes already lost.

The death and missing-person counts were changing and disagreed between reports. I am not treating those early totals as a settled accounting. The physical sequence, the revised records and the warning system's limitations are the parts we can follow more carefully.

## The signal did not fit the first explanation

An earthquake involves sudden slip on a fault. Its seismic record commonly includes identifiable arrivals of faster compressional P waves and slower shear S waves. Timing arrivals at multiple stations helps analysts estimate the source's location and depth.

The reviewed Nepal record was unusual. Its published origin fields included zero phases used and a standard error of 11.91. Those are properties of the catalog solution, not proof that every station on Earth recorded nothing. They need to be read alongside the agency's explanation of the long-period analysis.

A large falling mass applies a changing force to the ground. As it accelerates, turns and decelerates, the force on the Earth changes too. This can generate long, slow seismic waves, with periods of tens of seconds, quite different from the sharp arrivals that ordinary earthquake processing expects.

Göran Ekström and Colin Stark developed a way to recover landslide dynamics from such signals. Their paper, *Simple scaling of catastrophic landslide dynamics*, appeared in *Science* in 2013. They checked candidate events against satellite imagery and other evidence rather than treating a waveform alone as a complete story.

That distinction carries through to Nepal. USGS reviewers changed the event type after inspecting the evidence. GFZ reported a magnitude of 5.7 using a different analysis. Those magnitude values describe the recorded seismic signal through particular methods; they do not turn the source back into a tectonic fault rupture.

## Rock, glacier ice and the river below

Langtang Lirung rises a little over 7,200 meters, about 23,700 feet. The USGS describes high-grade metamorphic bedrock, including gneiss, quartzite and marble. Hard rock can hold a steep face, and a steep face can support glacier ice.

Early imagery-based estimates described a detached section about 610 meters across and a fall of roughly 1,200 meters. Those dimensions were provisional. A width and a fall distance do not by themselves establish the volume or the proportion of ice in the moving mass.

The initial USGS assessment left open whether rock failed first and carried glacier ice with it, or whether ice failure pulled rock away. Subsequent reporting quoted Dan Shugar and Jakob Steiner describing bedrock failure that took part of the glacier with it. Keeping that revision visible is better than preserving the early label as settled fact.

Dave Petley, writing in *The Landslide Blog* at *Eos* on August 26, found no evidence that a pre-existing glacial lake outburst initiated the disaster. Rock and ice falling together can fragment, generate frictional heat and contribute meltwater as the mass transforms into a debris flow.

Satellite images assembled by Shugar showed an intact face on August 25 and a missing section with a downslope track on August 26. The physical scar and the seismic signal support each other. The later Kathmandu Post reconstruction also describes river blockage and release as part of the evolving cascade, distinct from a lake initiating the collapse.

## A second movement was found afterward

The second USGS event, us7000tc90, was assigned magnitude 4.2 at 11:45 a.m. local time. That was three hours and eight minutes after the first event.

It was identified during post-processing. The public note explained that the lack of high-frequency energy prevented a precise location, so analysts fixed its location to that of the initial landslide. That is an explicit limitation of the solution. We should not turn the shared catalog location into an independently measured second origin.

The distinction matters operationally. Finding the second event in the record establishes that another signal occurred. It does not establish that an alert reached anyone while people downstream were already dealing with the first flood.

Nepal and China had flood-warning arrangements for the river system. The early account described monitoring designed around monsoon flooding and upstream stations destroyed by the flow. The later ICIMOD account adds a coverage problem: the avalanche originated in Nepal, outside the reach of the Chinese early-warning system.

These explanations concern different parts of a warning chain: the hazard being watched, the area covered, the survival of the sensors and the delivery of a message. A working rainfall or river-gauge system does not automatically detect a sudden high-mountain collapse.

Seven minutes between the reported initial movement and arrival at one crossing is an event interval. It is not seven minutes of usable warning. Detection, interpretation, transmission and movement to safety would all have to fit inside whatever time remained.

## The same mechanism can reach infrastructure in series

The flow traveled close to 100 kilometers through a connected valley system. Hydropower plants occupied the same river corridor because steep terrain and flowing water are precisely what makes those sites useful for generation.

Early reporting identified Rasuwagadhi, Chilime, Trishuli 3A, Trishuli 3B, Trishuli and Devighat among affected facilities. About 431 megawatts of operating capacity was reported offline, with a further 470 megawatts under construction damaged. The Kathmandu Post's hydropower reporting notes that capacity estimates varied and remained provisional.

The comparable event at Chamoli occurred on February 7, 2021. About 27 million cubic meters of rock and glacier ice fell from Ronti Peak, producing a flow that destroyed two hydropower projects and left more than 200 people dead or missing.

Shugar and colleagues reconstructed that sequence in *Science* in 2021, in *A massive rock and ice avalanche caused the 2021 disaster at Chamoli, Indian Himalaya*. It supplies a documented comparison, not a volume estimate that can be transferred to Nepal.

Mount Rainier provides another reason to think about the whole chain. USGS lahar monitoring is designed around flows from the volcano into populated valleys. The relevant local questions are which flow hazards have been mapped, what the sensors detect, and where the designated evacuation route reaches high ground. A route along the valley may remain in the path of the flow.

## What people asked

**Where did so much water come from if no glacial lake started it?** This was a recurring objection. A debris flow contains water as well as sediment and boulders. Melting ice can contribute water, and the mass can interact with river water, blockage and release downstream. The material here does not quantify each contribution. The appearance of a watery flood at the border is not evidence by itself that a glacial lake initiated the event.

**Where is the complete security-camera footage?** Several readers asked for links or the uninterrupted recording. The collected material refers to released clips and satellite reconstruction, but it does not establish that a complete public CCTV archive exists. I cannot fill the missing footage with a claimed frame-by-frame sequence. The USGS records and paired satellite images provide independent evidence of the source.

What stays with me is the correction. People reviewed a fast automatic answer, found that the physical explanation did not hold, and changed the public record. That is useful scientific work. Making it useful to the valley during the event requires an additional system: one that recognizes the relevant hazard and reaches people while they still have time to move.
